Keeping typing safe around ligatures, wherever the split came from

What this PR is about

Problem

In fonts that join letters together — Andika, Bloom’s default, and Charis both join ff, fl and ffl — letters the user has typed sometimes stop being drawn, and the caret sometimes stops moving as they arrow across those letters. The text is all there and saves correctly; only the screen is wrong, and it comes back on a font change or a page reload. The first fix for this shipped in 6.5, and the reporter still sees it on 6.5.1322, about 1% of the time when typing just after a ligature.

Measured on a running Bloom: 126 of 564 ink pixels missing, layout width unchanged. Only a screenshot catches this — the DOM and the inspector both look right.

Cause

Chromium drops glyphs from a paragraph whose text sits in two adjacent text nodes: it shapes the paragraph as a single run and then hands the glyphs out per text node, so a ligature spanning the boundary loses glyphs — not when the split happens, but at the next edit, which for the user is simply the next letter they type. The caret misbehaves in the same state, staying drawn in one place for two or three arrow presses.

The first fix repaired the splits Bloom itself caused, with ckeditor bookmarks, and stopped taking those bookmarks for ordinary typing. But bookmarks are not the only thing that splits a paragraph: Chromium’s own backspace in the middle of a word splits it, long-press composes its character into a text node of its own, and ckeditor takes bookmarks of its own for Enter, paste and the style commands. None of those were ever put back together.

What the PR does

  • The repair no longer depends on Bloom having caused the split: mergeTextNodesSplitByBookmarks becomes mergeAdjacentTextNodes, and its comment names the other causes.
  • It runs on keydown, before the keystroke lands (toolbox.ts:287), so the character arrives in a paragraph that is one whole text node again. This is the part that keeps the letters on screen — the existing half-second sweep runs after the damage.
  • That half-second sweep (toolbox.ts:1775) now runs unconditionally, catching the splits no keystroke follows: a menu paste, an IME, an undo, a long-press the user doesn’t type after.
  • The merge stands down where ckeditor is holding a “filling char” — a text node ckeditor tracks and will come back to edit. It asks ckeditor rather than searching the text for a zero-width space (some scripts use one in real text), ignores a reference ckeditor has been left holding to a node no longer in the box, and stands down only for the paragraph the char is in.
  • Both entry points do nothing unless the box really is split: 0.056 ms to check on a 2000-text-node box.

Worth knowing

  • Verified by driving real keystrokes, not by reasoning. The classic sequence, the same in a second paragraph, over a non-breaking space, inside Talking Book sentence spans, across a bold boundary, with the Leveled Reader tool open, and the long-press path: no stale paint anywhere, and the caret lands on the right character every time. A live Range over the merged text — how the reader tools and Talking Book paint their marks — comes through on exactly the same characters, and a selection spanning the split survives with typing replacing exactly what was selected.
  • Two paths are guarded but not exercised by hand. The repair stands down during an IME composition and while the long-press popup is open. The long-press case was checked by picking an accented letter with the mouse; picking it with its keyboard shortcut, and any real IME (Keyman), were not driven — synthetic key events don’t reach those handlers.
  • Not tried in a right-to-left or complex script. The same Chromium cluster-shaping problem could bite differently in Arabic, Thai or Devanagari, where clusters are the norm rather than the exception. Nothing about the fix is script-specific, but nothing about it was tested there either.
  • The Talking Book tool now re-checks its highlights a little more often. Rejoining a paragraph is a change Talking Book watches for, so on a keystroke that actually repairs a split it also runs its highlight repair-in-place pass. That pass is designed to run before the screen is painted, and the reader and Talking Book marks were verified intact by hand — but if highlight flicker is ever reported, this is the new path to look at.
  • The 1% is a mechanism, not a reproduction of Steve’s exact case. What is proved is that unrepaired splits drop glyphs and freeze the caret, that Bloom left them unrepaired, and that both symptoms match his description precisely. Whether every instance he hit came through this door can only be settled by him hammering the next build.
Keep the new guard that skips the repair while ckeditor is holding a “filling char”?

Ckeditor sometimes parks an invisible character (a zero-width space) in the text box so the blinking cursor stays visible — for instance when you click just next to bold text. It keeps a private handle on the little piece of text holding that character, so it can take the character out again later. If Bloom’s repair joins that piece into its neighbour first, ckeditor is left writing to something that is no longer in the document, and the invisible character stays in the user’s text — which is the problem behind BL-16490.

Devin raised this hazard on the last round and you decided to leave it alone. The reason to revisit it is that the repair now runs on every keystroke rather than only on a pause, so it meets that situation far more often.

Devin then found two things wrong with my first attempt at the guard, both fixed: it treated a reference ckeditor had been left holding to a piece of text no longer in the box as a reason to keep out, which could have left letters unpainted indefinitely; and it gave up on the whole text box rather than just the paragraph the character is in. On its third point — does the guard fire often enough to matter? — I measured rather than argued: the state occurred on 0 of 90 keystrokes across Talking Book sentence spans, a bold boundary and plain text, and could not be produced at all by parking the cursor in the five places ckeditor's own rule describes. So the guard costs nothing observable — and when it does fire, that paragraph is simply repaired on the next keystroke.

Should Bloom stop drawing ligatures while you are editing?

There is a blunt cure for this whole family of bugs: tell the browser not to join letters at all in the editing view (font-variant-ligatures: none). Text editors do this routinely — CodeMirror was asked to disable ligatures in its default stylesheet precisely because they cause “strange behavior when moving the cursor, selecting, etc.” Chromium’s own text documentation explains why: neither the shaper nor the code drawing the text knows where the individual letters inside a joined glyph sit.

It only changes appearance — the text, the saved book, and the printed PDF would be unaffected. But it means the edit view no longer shows the reader what the page will actually look like, in a product whose default font ligates and whose users care about how their script is rendered. I would not do it as part of this fix, but you may want it in your back pocket if the reports continue.

How do we find out whether this really was Steve’s 1%?

Steve says a reliable reproduction may not exist, and I could not reproduce his exact case either — what I could do was prove the mechanism and show that both of his symptoms (letters not drawn, and the caret refusing to move over them) come out of it. If he still sees it after this ships, the useful question will be “was the paragraph split at the time?”, and today nothing would tell us.
