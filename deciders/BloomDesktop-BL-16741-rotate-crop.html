Turning a cropped page picture

BL-16741 · branch BL-16741-rotate-image · PR 8227 · two decisions about what Rotate right should do to a crop.

The situation

A page can have a background picture. It fills the whole picture area of the page, and because it fills that area it cannot be turned as a box: turning the box would turn the page area itself. So the Rotate right command turns the picture inside the box instead.

The picture can also be cropped. Bloom stores a crop as four numbers on the picture: how big to draw the whole picture, and how far to move it up and to the left. The page area then shows one rectangle of the drawn picture, and the rest hangs outside and is hidden. So a crop says two things: which part of the picture you framed, and how much you magnified it.

Today the Rotate right command throws those four numbers away. The reason I gave for that was wrong. A rectangle of a picture turns perfectly well, and if you crop out everything but the giraffe, there is no reason you cannot then turn the giraffe.

What does make a choice unavoidable

The page area keeps its shape. On the page I measured in the Bloom running from this worktree it is 469 by 703 pixels, taller than it is wide. A crop fills that area, so your cropped rectangle has that same shape. Turn it a quarter turn and it now measures 703 by 469, which is wider than it is tall. The page area has not changed. The turned rectangle and the page area cannot coincide unless the page area is square.

So something must give: either the page shows less of what you framed, or the page is not completely filled, or the crop goes. Those are the three options.

The example these pictures use

A photograph of a giraffe that the camera saved on its side. The author cropped in on the giraffe's neck and body, magnifying it about two and a half times, and framed it left of the middle of the photograph. Every page shape below is drawn to the real 469 by 703 proportion, and every region is drawn by the same arithmetic the code uses, so the pictures are to scale.

The crop fills the page, and the animal is framed the way the author wanted. It is lying on its side, which is what Rotate right is for.

What each option does

All three turn the giraffe upright. They differ in what the page shows afterwards. In each row, the left picture marks on the photograph what the page ends up showing, and the right picture is the page.

Option 1: keep the middle and the magnification

The page stays completely full and the animal stays the size the author chose. The page keeps the same centre, so it is still the same picture of the same thing.

The cost is at the edges. Strips at what are now the top and the bottom of your framing drop off the page, and strips that your crop had cut off come back on at the sides.

Option 2: keep the whole crop, and accept blank bands

Nothing you framed is lost. The whole of it is turned and shrunk until it fits, so the page shows it at two thirds the size with a blank band above and another below.

On the page I measured, each band is about 195 pixels, which is more than a quarter of the height of the page.

Option 3 what it does today

The crop goes, so the page goes back to the middle of the whole photograph at no magnification at all. The author's framing and their zoom are both lost, and the blank bands are exactly the same as in option 2.

The green outline on the left shows why this feels arbitrary: the page ends up showing a region the author never chose, which happens to sit near the centre of the file.

Three things the pictures show that are easy to miss

  • Options 2 and 3 always leave exactly the same blank bands. That is not a coincidence of these numbers. A crop always fills the page area, so the crop has the page area's shape, and fitting the turned crop and fitting the turned page area are the same sum. The two options differ only in what the page shows inside that rectangle: your framing, or the middle of the file.
  • Today's turn already leaves blank bands, even when nothing is cropped. The code shrinks by the smaller of the two ratios of the page area's own sides, which fits the picture inside rather than filling. On the page I measured, a turn shrinks the picture to 67 per cent and leaves a band of about 195 pixels at the top and another at the bottom. That is question 2, and it is separate from the crop.
  • Only a page background is affected. For an ordinary picture on top of the page, Rotate right turns the box, not the picture inside it, so the crop is never touched and none of this arises.

What each option costs to build

OptionWorkRisk
Option 1 About 25 lines in rotateImageContentRight, replacing the call that clears the four numbers with arithmetic that recomputes them, plus four or five unit tests. The fiddly part is that the browser turns the picture about the centre of its own layout box, while those four numbers place that box, so the two have to be composed in the right order. A sign error puts the page on the wrong part of the photograph. There is also a case to handle: a crop taken from the very edge of a picture, where the wider region would run off the edge and has to be pushed back inside. The module now has tests, which is what makes this tractable.
Option 2 About 15 lines and the same tests. Simpler than option 1, because the whole crop is kept and only one scale factor is needed. Low. The result is easy to check by eye.
Option 3 None. It is what the code does now. The comment that justifies it is wrong and needs rewriting whichever option you choose. None.

What should a turn do to a crop?

The three rows on the left. Whichever you choose, the wrong reason currently written in three places has to be corrected: the comment on removeCropping in src/BloomBrowserUI/bookEdit/js/imageContentTransform.ts, the fix summary in the pull request description, and the "known and deliberate" list in the test notes on the card.

Notes

And when there is no crop at all?

This is the plainest case: an author puts a sideways photograph on a page and presses Rotate right, having cropped nothing. Today the picture is turned upright and shrunk until the whole of it fits, so on the page I measured it ends up two thirds the size, with a blank band of about 195 pixels above it and another below.

The alternative is to fill the page: turn the picture upright and magnify it until it covers the page area, which cuts off the ends of the photograph. That is what Bloom already does when a background picture is first put on a page, so it would make the turn behave like the rest of the feature. If you choose option 1 above, the same arithmetic gives you this for free.
